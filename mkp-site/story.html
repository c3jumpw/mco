The long version

Deconstructing things since I could pick up a device.

My family is from Sierra Leone, West Africa, and I have spent most of my life in Atlanta. What started as a fascination with technology became a career as a freelancer, and from there into building companies.

The short version is that I kept taking things apart. First software, then whole businesses. The long version is not a straight line. It has a fifty dollar website in it, a six hundred dollar check, two separate rebuilds, one detour that cost nearly everything, and a set of lessons that only arrive once you have paid for them.

It is worth reading if you are deciding whether to work with me, because the failures are the part that qualifies me.

Before any of it was a business

I was a kid with a computer and an internet connection, and that turned out to be the whole education. I would see something on television, research it online, find a free tool, and build my own version. That one pattern led down more paths than I can count.

Miniature films, so I learned video editing. A video game, so I learned to program. Three dimensional models, so I learned architecture and animation. Graphics and presentations. Excel formulas, web pages, WiFi networks, troubleshooting, emulators. Before I turned sixteen I had lost count of the tools I had taught myself.

By my teens I had become the person the family called when something stopped working.

Someone else saw it first

In college, a longtime friend asked me to join him in his clothing brand. I did not yet know that what I could do had any value. He did.

That business taught me everything a developer never learns alone: sales, marketing, relationship building, persuasion, logistics, leadership. I learned to cold sell. I learned to speak in public by hosting events. We ran productions, partnerships and business trips.

I was obsessed with it, to the detriment of my studies. I can still picture walking into lectures with a notebook full of notes that had nothing to do with the course.

Eventually I grew out of the role, and differences over where the business was heading ended it. I left something I had helped build, and that stung for a while. Then life moved on.

A library in Atlanta, winter 2014

Home for the winter break and drifting, I ended up in a library for reasons I no longer remember. I found The Ultimate Sales Machine on a shelf. That book triggered everything.

I read it, read it again, took notes, and it led me to another, and then another. My scattered skills suddenly had connecting glue, and I could finally picture what success in business actually looked like from the inside.

My talent was not to be a puzzle piece in someone else's business. It was to find the pieces and put them together.

Unywebs, and a fifty dollar website

I convinced my dad to register a GoDaddy account, and in the winter of 2014 I built Unywebs from scratch. The name was one I had coined for childhood projects. My family gave it the pat on the head reserved for cute little projects. My passion did not require their validation.

Then I went door to door through a small college town pitching web design. I was terrible at it. I was rejected by very nearly everyone.

Just as I was ready to give up, one owner said yes. He ran a small shop on the main strip, and he asked what a website would cost. I had never set a price, so I blurted out fifty dollars. When he handed me the cash I felt like I had won the lottery. That fifty dollars is the only reason I did not quit business altogether that week.

I went back years later to thank him for taking a chance on a skinny Black kid with no experience.

The six hundred dollar check

Door to door works badly when you go in blind, so I got smarter. Craigslist ads in nearby cities, printed cards, and eventually the realization that I had been pitching to houses full of people who were not business owners. I switched to office complexes.

In Decatur I was working my way through a building's tenants when I ran into the man who owned the building itself. He asked me to build him a website, and wrote a check for six hundred dollars as the deposit.

I had been charging seventy five. I screamed in my car, then delivered the whole site within a day. He was astonished that I had built an entire brand out of a short conversation and some vague information online. Taking minimal input and producing a complete identity is still the thing I do.

He asked what else I did. Graphics, then his listings, then more, until he hired me as his assistant.

The renaissance, and what broke it

That job is still the best one I ever had. A commercial real estate office, consistent income for the first time in my life, my own desk, and a front row seat to how a responsible owner actually operates. I was being paid to learn an industry while building my own thing in the same building, surrounded by tenants who were exactly the clients I wanted.

I was at two and three networking events a day. Referrals, tenants, ads, all of it compounding. I drove the worst car in the parking lot and parked it far away so nobody would see it.

Then I split my focus between school and the work, and the momentum went with it. Losing momentum costs more than losing a client. For the first time my clients saw results below par. I got worse at managing time, not better.

It took years to stop blaming the circumstances. It was a test every owner has to pass: changing conditions, less time, recovering on the bad days, staying efficient when nothing is easy.

The detour

Between 2017 and 2018 I took a detour into someone else's holding company. I became the right hand, the voice in the room, the one who presented and negotiated. I built their entire identity in a single day, and then spent months being told the payoff was coming.

It never came. I worked seventy hour weeks for a company that was not mine, unpaid, while my savings, my credit and my client list all went into it. My car finally gave out on the way to that office and never came back. I left with a duffel bag and a lesson.

Never build someone else's dream on your own credit.

Rebuilding, twice

I rebuilt from nothing twice. The first time was fast and shallow. Club flyers, music covers, restaurant menus, the peak of my graphic design years and the floor of my margins. Thirty five dollars a flyer is a good time. It is not a business.

The second rebuild held. Better clients, including a luxury restaurant that is still a client today. A mentor ten years ahead of me who taught me reputation, managing people, and treating credit as a tool rather than a rescue. I started saving ten percent of every dollar for the first time in my life.

To fund it I took a teller job at a bank, which turned into an education in standards and process and a reason to be ruthless with my hours. A forced schedule made me better than an open one ever had. I registered the businesses, repaired my credit, built real savings, and spent the surplus on conferences.

The window I watched close

For years I had been persuading owners to move their operations online, with limited success. Then 2020 arrived and the thing I had spent years selling became the default, at exactly the moment I was not set up to deliver it at scale.

I took on more than I could carry. Missed deadlines, refunds, and the particular exhaustion of working constantly without moving. I ran ten miles in zigzags to cover one.

Hiring too fast

When I finally left the job and went all in, I made the opposite mistake. I brought on several assistants at once, with better pay, better instructions and better training than any earlier attempt, and still got it wrong.

I hired too many people at once, and I had not yet mastered my own administration. You cannot delegate an order you do not have. I spent that year correcting other people's work instead of growing, and the client list thinned out again.

The lesson, paid for more than once: iterate long enough to grow before scaling.

Where it landed

These days the order is reversed. I fix the systems and the operations before anyone talks about marketing, because marketing a broken operation only makes the breakage arrive faster.

I take on specific projects where that sequence makes sense: better systems, better training, better personal and professional administration. Every practice gets run on my own companies before it is handed to anyone else.

The arsenal

Business planning and structuring. Hiring and recruiting. Company culture, systems creation, sequencing. Digital advertising, graphic design, web design and development, AI prompting, copywriting, marketing strategy. Door to door sales, cold calling, management, social media growth, forecasting, accounting, community management, public speaking, selling, closing.

Put to work across tax and financial services, ecommerce, real estate, fitness, restaurants, hospitality, lounges and clubs, healthcare, personal brands, nonprofits, transportation, apparel, education and daycare, technology companies and startups.

Each industry taught something different. Each failure added to the understanding. Each success confirmed a principle.

What I know now

  • Iterate long enough to grow before scaling too quickly. I learned this one the expensive way, several times. Hiring too fast, expanding too soon, building before the foundation was solid. Each time it cost clients, money and momentum.
  • Do not get so caught up in operations that you miss where the market is going. While I was perfecting my processes, competitors were building their names. One eye on the work, one eye on the horizon.
  • Know what your customers actually need. Not what you think they need, and not what you would like to sell them. That means listening more than talking and observing more than assuming.
  • Being the best is no longer enough on its own. People have to know it. Your digital presence is not optional, it is the credibility check. That is the lesson I wish I had learned a decade earlier, back when I was doing exceptional work that nobody outside my own network ever heard about.

Nowadays

The work finally has a name. What I do is business system design: working out how a business should actually function, finding what is getting in the way, and building a better way for it to operate. The tools are just how the thinking gets implemented.

It rests on five things I know I am good at.

  • Seeing how the parts connect. Sales, marketing, onboarding, follow up, fulfillment and administration are not separate activities, and treating them as though they are is what quietly breaks most businesses.
  • Turning vague into operational. Someone says they need to get organized, and I hand back workflows, pipelines, automations and procedures that a real team can actually use on a Monday.
  • Diagnosing past the symptom. More traffic rarely fixes a business that is not converting. The obvious solution is often not the right one, and I would rather question the offer and the customer journey than redesign a page and call it done.
  • Bridging what customers see and what happens behind it. I came up through branding and design as well as the operational technology, so I can hold both sides of the problem at once.
  • Seeing what an idea could become. It is why the ventures and the projects keep appearing. I rarely look at a brief without also seeing what it could grow into.

Put plainly: I help owners work out what is not working, fix the underlying problems, and build better ways of operating.

The next stretch is about judgment as much as systems. Not just making a business more organized, but knowing which problems are actually costing money, which ones deserve to be fixed first, and how to measure whether the fix worked. That is a better thing to be building toward than anything I was chasing ten years ago, and I have the scar tissue to do it properly this time.

True success is sitting in the shade of a tree someone planted years before, and making sure you are planting those trees for other people too.

The story is not finished. It is still being written, one decision and one client at a time, and the best chapters are still ahead.
